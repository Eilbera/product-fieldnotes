Choose what an experiment can prove before choosing its design

Learn why shared capacity and sustained learning can require different experiments. Then separate an investment budget from a credible delivery commitment.

Practice lesson

Separate service quality from sustained learning

Choose an assignment unit that matches the claim you need to test. Learn when a service-quality experiment cannot establish retention, and produce a design brief that limits the launch claim.

Evidence type: practitioner self report

The problem

Imagine changing both order assignment and the instructions workers see in a delivery marketplace. Leadership wants faster service and stronger worker retention. One experiment dashboard looks convenient, but the outcomes may require incompatible exposure patterns.

What the team tried first

DoorDash explains why ordinary order-level A/B assignment encounters interference: orders share an available driver fleet.[2] Assigning treatment to one order can therefore affect another order's outcome.[2] This is a documented design problem, not a reported failed trial with measured losses.

How the method works

Understand what a switchback changes

A switchback assigns variants to region-and-time units instead of individual deliveries.[2] The region receives different variants across time windows. DoorDash's original account randomizes each window rather than alternating deterministically after one initial assignment.[1]

Look for work carried into the next window

DoorDash illustrates a policy that leaves longer deliveries for control periods, making treatment appear faster without improving the algorithm.[1] Its later account warns that geographic and temporal boundaries can retain interference.[2] Smaller windows can increase the share of work near those boundaries.[2]

Separate repeated learning from immediate response

DoorDash says switchbacks cannot provide the consistent experience needed for its driver-learning and retention questions.[2] Persistent market assignment can address learning and shared-capacity interference, but the company reports insufficient power in many cases.[2] Many deliveries do not replace a sufficient number of independent assignment units.[2]

What the practitioners report doing

DoorDash describes driver-level assignment for retention and switchbacks for delivery quality.[2] The experiments run sequentially or in separate geographic areas, rather than simultaneously in the same markets.[2] One reported sequence finalizes the treatment through quality tests before beginning the longer retention experiment.[2]

Write the claims before the design

The following protocol is editorial synthesis, not a statistical analysis specification. Write the service outcome, worker outcome, and intended rollout population separately. Name whether each claim requires immediate exposure, sustained exposure, or an interaction between both.

Explain why the separation matters

A short service comparison and a sustained retention comparison are different evidence requests. Writing them separately prevents the easier measurement from silently replacing the harder launch requirement. Agree on the smallest worthwhile benefit and unacceptable harm before examining treatment results.

Draw the interference path

Identify who shares workers, inventory, queues, or other scarce capacity. Explain how assigning one participant could change another participant's outcome. This separates a plausible shared-resource problem from a product that is merely complicated.

Inspect transitions before setting windows

Ask engineering to trace unfinished work when variants change and movement across region boundaries. These observations inform window length and whether excluding transition periods is defensible. Exclusions change the population being measured, so the analyst should document their consequences.

Compare feasible designs against the decision

Ask analytics what effects each available assignment plan could meaningfully distinguish. A valid experiment can still be too imprecise to resolve the agreed benefit or harm threshold. More observations inside the same markets may not repair too few independent markets.

Protect the treatment identity

Record the dispatch policy, interface, eligibility rules, and exposure schedule for each test. If the intervention changes, identify which earlier conclusions require rechecking. Otherwise, a quality result for one version can silently validate a retention claim about another.

Choose the simpler design when the mechanism permits

An isolated screen change may need sustained individual assignment without switchbacks. A dispatch-model change without a learning requirement may suit region-time assignment. Use this comparison when shared resources or sustained exposure threaten the intended interpretation.

Avoid overstating separate results

Separate tests reduce uncertainty without necessarily proving the effect of their combined rollout. If learned behavior changes dispatch performance, that interaction needs explicit investigation. An inconclusive retention result also cannot establish no harm; ask which harmful effects remain compatible with the evidence.

Bring design into the launch discussion

This extends ordinary experiment planning by making the exposure pattern part of the product claim. Ask which desired claim the proposed design cannot establish before promising a launch readout. Local interaction evidence remains necessary when staged tests stand in for a combined rollout.

What the evidence supports

The sources document one company's design choices and reported operating sequence.[1][2] They support teaching the trade-off between interference, sustained learning, and statistical power. They do not establish an independently measured business gain from adopting this protocol.

What it does not establish

The engineering accounts have reputation and recruitment incentives; raw data and independent replication were not inspected. The 2022 article was read again during publication, while the original switchback details rely on preserved November 22 excerpts. These historical accounts do not provide a universal estimator, window length, or market-count requirement.

Hypothetical worked decision: batching plus a driver screen

Starting situation

All inputs below are invented. A marketplace proposes a batching policy plus an explanatory driver screen. Prototype feedback suggests repeated exposure is needed, and operations confirms shared local order queues.

Inputs

The team has a proposed launch, a shared-capacity map, and an unresolved learning requirement. Cross-region movement and unfinished batches have not been measured. No service or retention effect has been established.

Alternatives

Driver assignment preserves screen exposure but mixes policies within shared queues. Region-time assignment reduces simultaneous mixing but interrupts learning. Persistent market assignment could test the combined intervention if enough suitable markets support an informative comparison.

Decision

Withhold the combined rollout and approve design investigation. Engineering measures boundary crossings and unfinished batches; analytics compares assignment plans against agreed benefit and harm thresholds. The PM checks whether the screen merely explains behavior or changes how drivers respond to dispatch.

Resulting artifact

Design brief: measure late-order rate and worker retention over a pre-agreed horizon; prefer persistent market assignment if independence and precision are adequate. Otherwise, consider staged quality and retention tests with a fixed treatment version and explicitly restricted claims. The PM owns launch scope, analytics owns design adequacy, and operations owns constraints; a positive switchback result cannot authorize a retention-benefit claim.

Limits

Shared queues are observed only within this invented scenario; learning and boundary effects remain unresolved. Enough suitable independent markets could permit a combined test, while negligible interference could justify individual assignment. Evidence that learned behavior alters dispatch performance would weaken the case for interpreting separate tests as combined validation.

Foundation through a decision

An appetite is not an estimate

The original idea

Ryan Singer's Shape Up defines appetite as a time budget for a standard team size.[3] An estimate starts with a design and predicts effort; an appetite constrains which design is worth pursuing.[3] The method keeps time fixed while allowing scope to change.[3]

Where it goes wrong

Approving a budget does not establish a release date or technical feasibility. Shrinking required security, accessibility, or contractual behavior does not make an otherwise infeasible plan acceptable. Those requirements constrain the solution; they are not convenient scope reductions.

Hypothetical worked decision: a restricted bulk editor

Starting situation

All details below are invented. Customers repeatedly change the status of records they already control. Leadership will fund a short investigation, but engineering has not verified authorization across mixed record selections.

Inputs

The input is repeated status editing, not evidence that customers need a general bulk editor. Singer reports replacing a request for complex permissions with an archive warning after investigating the underlying problem.[3] That example concerns surprising consequences, not proof that warnings can replace authorization.

Alternatives

Choose between a general bulk editor, a restricted status-change tool, or investigation before committing delivery. Investigate the restricted tool because the known task does not establish a need for arbitrary fields or cross-account operations. Keep required authorization behavior intact.

Decision

Research first checks whether status changes cover the recurring task, which tests whether narrower scope still provides the benefit. Engineering tests authorization on mixed selections to establish whether the restricted operation is credible. Approve delivery only after useful scope and technical feasibility are supported.

Resulting artifact

Scope record: fund investigation of status changes inside one account; exclude arbitrary fields and cross-account edits. Require authorization for every selected record; the PM owns task coverage and engineering owns safe execution. Reopen scope or funding if necessary tasks require excluded fields or authorization needs redesign; do not promise a customer release date.

Limits

Willingness to invest remains distinct from likely effort; a delivery estimate becomes useful once a credible solution exists. The inspected chapter provides practitioner guidance, not comparative evidence or a reason to copy its team size and cycle length.[3] Team question: which statement names our investment limit, and which claims what we can safely deliver?
